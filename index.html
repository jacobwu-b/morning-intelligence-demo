Today's Thesis

Markets Are Pricing a War Nobody Can End Cleanly

On February 28, the U.S. and Israel launched joint strikes on Iran — killing its Supreme Leader and targeting its nuclear infrastructure. Iran has spent the week retaliating across the Middle East. The Strait of Hormuz, through which 20% of global oil flows, is now a war zone. Today, that reality collided with a labor market that was already quietly deteriorating — the third job loss in five months. Markets didn't panic. They did something more unsettling: they coldly repriced what the next six to twelve months looks like if this conflict doesn't resolve quickly.

What's Actually Driving This

Two Crises — One Old, One New. Neither Simple to Resolve.

The War — Primary Driver
A decade of nuclear tensions, resolved by force
The US-Iran conflict didn't start last week. It's the culmination of years of failed nuclear negotiations, proxy wars, and domestic unrest inside Iran. The Trump administration struck when Iran was at its weakest — mid-revolution, leadership fractured. Iran's retaliation is now spreading across six Gulf states.
Duration: weeks to months minimum. The regime is decapitated but not defeated. No ceasefire framework exists.
The Jobs Number — Secondary Driver
Noisy data masking a real underlying trend
February's -92,000 print had distortions: a Kaiser Permanente healthcare strike pulled out 30,000 jobs, and severe winter weather hit construction. But strip those out and the trend is still soft. December was quietly revised to -17,000. The labor market has now contracted in three of the last five months.
Part noise, part signal. The March report on April 3rd will be the true read — without the strike distortion.
The Core Dynamic

The Fed Has One Lever. The Problem Needs Two.

A war near the Strait of Hormuz is inherently inflationary — oil goes up, shipping costs go up, everything made or moved gets more expensive. A softening labor market is inherently deflationary — less hiring, slower consumer spending, slower growth. The Federal Reserve's entire toolkit is designed to fight one or the other. Cut rates to rescue the labor market, and you accelerate oil-driven inflation. Hold rates to fight inflation, and you let the economy bleed out. The Fed has no clean move. That policy paralysis is precisely what markets sold off today — not fear, but the cold calculation that the next twelve months just got materially harder to navigate.

Historical Precedent

The Playbook Exists. The Timeline Is the Variable.

1973
The Yom Kippur War triggered an Arab oil embargo. Oil quadrupled. The S&P lost 48% over the following two years — not because of the war, but because oil stayed elevated long after the fighting stopped. The economy entered a stagflationary recession that lasted nearly two years.
The market doesn't recover when the conflict ends. It recovers when oil comes down. Watch the commodity, not the headlines.
1990
Iraq invaded Kuwait. Oil spiked 130% in three months. The S&P fell 20%. But the Gulf War resolved in 100 days — oil crashed back, and the market recovered fully within six months of the invasion. The key variable was how quickly the oil supply disruption ended.
When the conflict is geographically contained and resolved fast, markets recover sharply. Speed of resolution is everything.
2022
Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent oil to $120. The Fed, already behind on inflation, couldn't cut its way out. The S&P fell 25%, Nasdaq fell 35% over nine months. Energy was the only sector with positive returns that entire year.
When oil drives the selloff, money doesn't leave markets — it rotates. Out of technology and growth. Into energy, utilities, and consumer staples. That rotation started today.
Directional Read

This is a war story, not a jobs story. The jobs number added fuel — but the Strait of Hormuz is the actual variable that determines how long and how deep this goes. The historical range is wide: a fast resolution looks like 1990 — painful but brief. A prolonged conflict looks like 1973 — a multi-year reset. The market today is pricing somewhere between the two. Either way, the rotation out of growth and into defensives has already begun. The question is duration, not direction.

Scenario A — Ceasefire within 30 days: correction contained, sharp recovery likely.
Scenario B — Conflict drags through Q2: stagflation risk rises materially, 20–35% drawdown range.
